# Blog post retention - the spec

How to keep someone reading. Built from ~60 sources in August 2026: NN/g eye-tracking, Chartbeat's 2-billion-visit dataset, Princeton's GEO paper, and the psychology literature on curiosity and narrative.

`/blog-post` writes to this file. `/audit` grades against it.

Every rule below is tagged **[E]** evidence-backed or **[C]** practitioner convention. Never present a [C] rule as fact.

---

# The numbers you are writing against

**People read a fifth of what you write. [E]**
On an average page users read at most 28% of the words, and 20% is the realistic figure. Each extra 100 words buys 4.4 more seconds of attention, which is about 18 words of actual reading. Adding length does not add reading.

**Half your readers never reach the halfway mark. [E]**
Median scroll depth is roughly 50%. On Slate only 25% got past the 1,600th pixel, and 5-10% never scrolled a single pixel.

**55% of pageviews get under 15 seconds. [E]**
Departure risk peaks in the first 10 seconds and only flattens around 30.

**But survive 30 seconds and they are probably staying. [E]**
99% of pages show "negative aging" - a reader past the first 30 seconds is likely to stay 2 minutes or more. **This is why the opening deserves more work than the rest of the post combined.**

**The compound effect is the biggest number in this whole file. [E]**
NN/g rewrote the same content three ways and measured usability: concise +58%, scannable +47%, objective non-promotional language +27%. All three together: **+124%.** These stack.

# The opening

**1. The payoff appears in the first 10 seconds. [E]**
Not the first 10 paragraphs. If the value is not visible before the bail-out spike, nothing else in the post matters.

**2. Inverted pyramid. Answer first, then reasoning, then background. [E]**
NN/g tested it directly: readers found the main point immediately and the rewritten versions used half the words. Never build suspense before the answer in search-driven content.

**3. Make that answer a standalone 40-50 word block. [E]**
That is where paragraph featured snippets cluster, and it is one skimmable screen unit. It also has to work alone, because roughly one reader in ten never scrolls.

**4. Delete your first paragraph, then check whether the second one was the real opening. [C]**
Throat-clearing is a warm-up the writer needs and the reader does not.

**5. Banned openings. [E, adjacent]**
"In today's world", "in the fast-paced digital landscape", defining a term the reader just searched for. NN/g's promotional "marketese" version scored worst on nearly every usability measure; objective language alone was worth +27%.

**6. Open a SPECIFIC information gap, not a vague one. [E]**
Loewenstein's information-gap theory: curiosity fires on a known, salient, closable gap. "The results may surprise you" creates nothing. "One item on this list voids your warranty" creates a real one.

**7. Front-load every unit - page, section, paragraph, sentence. [E]**
79% of users scan. Readers skip a second idea inside a paragraph because it is not in the opening sentence. One idea per paragraph, idea first.

**8. The first two or three words of a line are the highest-value real estate on the page. [E]**
The F-pattern's vertical scan runs down the left edge. Starting sentences with "However" or "In order to" wastes it.

# Structure and formatting

**9. Subhead every 150-300 words. [E for the pattern, C for the interval]**
The layer-cake pattern - readers jumping heading to heading - is the most effective scanning mode there is. One publisher lifted engaged time about 20% by adding subheads alone.

**10. The subhead stack must tell the whole story on its own. [E]**
Delete the body, read only the subheads: is that a coherent summary? If not, rewrite them. Put the information-carrying word first - if a reader sees only two words, those two must carry the gist.

**11. Clever subheads lose to clear ones. [E]**
Vague or cute headings collapse the layer-cake pattern back into the worse F-pattern, and anything that looks like an ad gets banner-blinded.

**12. Sentences average 14-17 words. Past 25 is a rewrite. [E]**
Comprehension runs near 100% at 8 words, about 90% at 14, and falls off a cliff past 21.

**13. Paragraphs: one idea, two to four lines. [C, derived from E]**

**14. Line length 50-75 characters, 66 optimal. Never full-bleed text. [E]**

**15. Bullets for genuinely parallel items only. [E]**
Users examine about 70% of a bulleted list versus 55% of paragraph text. But bulleting chopped-up prose is the cargo cult version and just fragments the argument.

**16. Bold one or two phrases per section. [E for effect, C for the count]**
The mechanism is visual contrast, which dies at high density. Bolding everything bolds nothing.

**17. Every image must carry information. [E]**
Eye-tracking shows readers scrutinise photos with real content and completely ignore decorative stock imagery. A generic hero image is a spacer.

**18. Put the second-most-important thing at the midpoint, not the end. [E]**
Median scroll is 50%. Everything below that reaches a minority.

# The middle

**19. Distribute the retention devices. Attrition is continuous, not one cliff. [E]**

**20. Close every loop you open within a few hundred words. [E, with a correction]**
The famous Zeigarnik "unfinished things are memorable" claim did not survive meta-analysis - the effect vanishes once Zeigarnik's own 1927 data is removed. What DID hold is the resumption effect: about two-thirds of people resume an interrupted task. So an open loop creates a pull to continue **now**, not a memory later. Pay it off fast.

**21. Ask the reader to predict before you reveal. [E]**
Explicitly generating a prediction raises curiosity and improves learning of surprising outcomes. "Guess what percentage converted" before the number.

**22. State the consensus before you break it. [E]**
Surprise only produces the memory and attention boost when a prediction existed first. A contrarian claim lands harder after you name what everyone believes.

**23. Concrete beats abstract, everywhere, always. [E]**
Dual-coding: concrete words are stored as language AND image. Across 15.5M tweets and 50,000 Reddit posts, one standard unit of concreteness was worth roughly 216 more upvotes. Concrete wording also makes identical claims feel truer. **Replace every category noun with the specific instance.**

**24. Write in scenes, not points. [E]**
Narrative form produces higher processing fluency than exposition, and fluency - not emotional identification - is what mediates persuasion. Transported readers stop critically evaluating and stop leaving.

**25. End sections on tension, not resolution. [E]**
Readers given a cliffhanger section ending wanted the next part more than those given a clean resolution, even when enjoyment ratings were identical. Apply at every section break, not just the intro.

**26. Bucket brigades between blocks. [C]**
"Here's the problem with that." "But the numbers said something else." Ordinary transitions connect ideas; these create tension so the reader cannot stop at a paragraph boundary.

**27. Cut mid-article digressions. [E]**
Every sidebar, definition box and tangent is a fresh decision point where the reader can leave. Move it to the end or cut it.

# The ending

**28. The mid-article anchor-text link is your real CTA. The footer box is not. [E]**
HubSpot's own blog study: anchor-text CTAs inside paragraphs drove **47-93%** of each post's leads. End-of-post banner CTAs drove **6%**. This is the single most actionable finding in this file.

**29. Still close hard, because whoever reached the end is your highest-intent reader. [E]**
66% of engaged time happens below the fold.

**30. Two in-body CTAs, roughly a third and two-thirds down, each matching the paragraph it sits in. [C]**

**31. The last 100 words carry disproportionate weight. [E, applied by convention]**
Peak-end rule: people judge an experience by its most intense moment and its final moment, not the average. Make the ending a deliberate high point, not an admin block.

**32. Never "In conclusion". [C]**
It tells the reader nothing new follows, which throws away the ending as a persuasion slot. Close with a reframe, one next action, an open question, or a cliffhanger.

**33. Exactly ONE "read this next" link. Not a grid of nine. [E]**
Single-page visitors return within 7 days 8% of the time. Two-page visitors, 22%. One extra pageview makes a reader 2.75x likelier to come back.

# Writing for AI citation without killing the prose

**34. There is no conflict. This is the important one. [E]**
NN/g proved decades ago that humans want the answer first, and answer-first is exactly what makes a passage citable. **What kills AI citation is not personality - it is unresolved pronouns, sections that only make sense in sequence, and zero original numbers.** The jokes are safe. The vagueness is not.

**35. Open every H2 with a self-contained 40-60 word answer, then expand. [E]**
Retrieval works on 100-300 word chunks, not pages. Each section must survive being lifted alone.

**36. No orphan pronouns at the start of a section. Re-state the subject by name. [E]**
"The company's revenue grew 3%" is useless once retrieved without its page.

**37. Statistics, quotations and named sources are the highest-leverage change for AI visibility. [E]**
The Princeton and Georgia Tech GEO paper measured up to **+40% visibility** in generative engine responses from adding exactly these.

**38. Schema markup does NOT drive AI citation. [E, negative result]**
Ahrefs tracked 1,885 pages adding JSON-LD against ~4,000 controls: no meaningful citation growth, and a 4.6% decline in AI Overviews. Keep schema for classic SEO. Do not sell it as an AI lever.

**39. Ranking no longer guarantees citation. [E]**
Top-10 organic results were 76% of AI Overview citations in July 2025 and 38% by March 2026. Structure is now doing independent work.

**40. Being cited is the new click. [E]**
AI Overviews cut organic CTR 61% overall - but brands cited *inside* the overview earn about 35% higher CTR and roughly 120% more clicks per impression than uncited brands on the same query.

**41. Freshness is weighted heavily. Date the page and update it. [E]**
AI-cited content runs about 26% fresher than traditional organic results.

# The humour trade-off, stated honestly

Humour is the default in this repo and the rule is in CLAUDE.md. One finding belongs here as a counterweight.

**Eisend's meta-analysis of 369 correlations [E]:** humour has a large effect on attention and positive affect (r = 0.416), roughly twice as much effect on attitude-toward-the-content as attitude-toward-the-business - and it **significantly reduces perceived source credibility.**

So humour buys attention and likeability and costs some authority. That is a real trade, not a free win. Which is why:

- **Proof blocks, prices, FAQ answers and the extractable opener stay straight.** They are carrying the credibility that the humour is spending.
- **The prose between them goes full standup.** That is where retention is won and nothing is being lifted verbatim.

**Related [E]:** what drives sharing is physiological arousal, not positivity. Awe, anger and anxiety travel; contentment and sadness do not. A calm, pleasant, useful post is the worst-sharing profile there is.

# Myths - never repeat these

**"Human attention span is 8 seconds, less than a goldfish."** Traced to a Microsoft Canada report that never contains the number. The source could not produce evidence when pressed, and the goldfish figure has no scientific basis either.

**"Dwell time is a Google ranking factor."** Denied specifically and repeatedly by Google. The term came from a Bing blog post. What IS real is NavBoost, which aggregates query-level click data including a `lastLongestClick` field - but that is not your Analytics time-on-page and you cannot optimise it directly. Make the page satisfy the click; do not chase the metric.

**"Longer content ranks better."** Semrush puts the length-to-position correlation at 0.02, which is noise, and Backlinko found word count evenly distributed across positions 1-10. Long content earns more backlinks (77% more above 3,000 words), and the links do the work. Length is a marker of investment, not a cause of ranking.

**"Whitespace increases comprehension 20%."** A fabricated citation. The real study (Chaparro 2004) found margins improved speed and comprehension - use that one.

**"Shares mean people read it."** Chartbeat found essentially no relationship between share volume and engaged reading time. The most-engaged article in their sample had under 100 likes.

**"7,000-word posts get 4x the traffic."** Vendor data with severe selection bias - 7,000-word posts are rare pillar pages that receive disproportionate promotion and link building.

# The test, run on every draft

1. **Read only the subheads.** Do they tell the whole story? If not, rewrite them.
2. **Is the answer in the first 40-50 words**, and does it stand alone if nothing else is read?
3. **Could any section be lifted out and still make sense?** Any orphan pronouns at a section start?
4. **How many original numbers are in this?** Zero means it will not be cited.
5. **Where does a reader bail?** Find the paragraph and put a device there.
6. **Is there an anchor-text CTA inside the prose**, not just a box at the bottom?
7. **Read it aloud. Would a real person keep reading this at 11pm?**
